There are many ways to build a qubit, and each one is a trade-off. Superconducting circuits are fast but need fridges colder than outer space. Trapped ions are extremely accurate but slow. Topological qubits could protect themselves, if they turn out to be real.

21.1What makes a good quantum computer: DiVincenzo's criteria

In 2000, David DiVincenzo listed what a quantum computer needs:

  1. Scalable, well-characterised qubits
  2. The ability to initialise qubits to a simple state (like |00…0⟩)
  3. Long coherence times relative to gate times
  4. A universal set of gates
  5. Qubit-specific measurement

Plus two for quantum communication:

  1. The ability to convert between stationary qubits and "flying" qubits (photons)
  2. The ability to faithfully transmit flying qubits between locations

Every platform below meets these to different degrees.

21.2Superconducting qubits

How they work: tiny circuits made of superconducting metal (aluminium, niobium, tantalum) on a silicon or sapphire chip. A Josephson junction, two superconductors separated by a nanometre-thin insulator, acts as a non-linear inductor. This makes the circuit's energy levels unevenly spaced, so the lowest two can be used as |0⟩ and |1⟩. The most common design is the transmon (2007). Qubits are controlled with microwave pulses (about 4–8 GHz) and cooled to about 10 millikelvin in a dilution refrigerator.

✅ Pros
  • Very fast gates (tens of nanoseconds)
  • Fabricated with semiconductor-style techniques
  • Most mature software and cloud ecosystem
  • Proven below-threshold error correction
❌ Cons
  • Short coherence times
  • Need extreme cryogenics
  • Wiring bottleneck: each qubit needs control lines from room temperature
  • Fixed, mostly nearest-neighbour connectivity; every qubit is slightly different

Who: IBM (Heron; Nighthawk, 120 qubits, 2025; Loon, a qLDPC test chip; Condor, 1,121 qubits, 2023), Google (Sycamore, 53 qubits, 2019; Willow, 105 qubits, 2024), Rigetti, IQM, AWS (Ocelot), Alice & Bob, USTC in China (Zuchongzhi series), Fujitsu/RIKEN, OQC, and others.

🤓 Did you know?

Superconducting qubits run at about 10 millikelvin and are steered by microwave pulses of about 4–8 GHz.

21.3Trapped ions

How they work: individual atoms with an electron removed (ions of ytterbium, barium, calcium, beryllium…) are held in place in a vacuum by electric fields, often on microfabricated chip traps. Qubits are stored in the ions' internal energy levels. Lasers or microwaves perform gates. The ions' shared vibrational motion (phonons) acts as a "bus" that entangles them.

✅ Pros
  • Every ion is identical (made by nature)
  • Highest gate fidelities (two-qubit gates above 99.9%)
  • Very long coherence (seconds or more)
  • All-to-all connectivity within a trap
❌ Cons
  • Slow gates (microseconds to milliseconds)
  • Scaling beyond a few hundred ions per trap is hard
  • Complex laser systems (though some designs use microwaves and electronics instead)

Scaling strategies: shuttle ions between zones on a chip (the QCCD architecture, used by Quantinuum), or link traps with photons.

Who: Quantinuum (H1, H2, and Helios, 98 qubits, launched 2025), IonQ (which acquired Oxford Ionics in 2025), AQT, Quantum Art, eleQtron and others.

21.4Neutral atoms

How they work: uncharged atoms (rubidium, caesium, strontium, ytterbium) are cooled to microkelvin temperatures and held in arrays of tightly focused laser beams called optical tweezers. Two-qubit gates use Rydberg states, in which an electron is excited so far from the nucleus that the atom becomes huge and strongly interacts with neighbours (the Rydberg blockade). Atoms can be physically moved mid-computation, giving flexible connectivity.

✅ Pros
  • Large arrays: thousands of atoms trapped (Caltech held over 6,100 in 2025)
  • Identical qubits; good coherence
  • Reconfigurable connectivity; highly parallel gates
  • Leading demonstrations of many logical qubits
❌ Cons
  • Slower cycle times, especially measurement
  • Atom loss requires reloading (continuous reloading was demonstrated in 2025)
  • Two-qubit fidelities still catching up with ions (now above 99.5%)

Who: QuEra (with Harvard and MIT), Pasqal, Atom Computing (partnered with Microsoft), Infleqtion, planqc.

21.5Photonic quantum computers

How they work: qubits are encoded in particles of light: polarisation, path, time-bin, or quantum states of light modes. Photons barely interact with their environment (good for coherence), but also barely interact with each other (bad for two-qubit gates). Entangling operations are done probabilistically, using measurement, and the computer is built as a measurement-based or fusion-based machine that constantly generates and measures entangled photon clusters.

✅ Pros
  • Much of the hardware works at room temperature (single-photon detectors are usually cryogenic)
  • Natural fit for networking
  • Manufacturable in existing semiconductor foundries
  • Very fast
❌ Cons
  • Photon loss is the dominant error
  • Entangling gates are probabilistic, requiring huge numbers of components
  • Large-scale fault-tolerant designs are still unproven

Who: PsiQuantum (building large facilities in Brisbane and Chicago), Xanadu (Borealis, 2022; Aurora, a modular networked machine, 2025), Quandela, ORCA, QuiX, and USTC (Jiuzhang boson-sampling machines).

21.6Silicon spin qubits

How they work: single electrons (or holes) trapped in quantum dots, tiny structures in silicon similar to transistors. The electron's spin (up or down) is the qubit. Controlled by voltages and microwaves.

✅ Pros
  • Tiny (~100 nm), so potentially millions per chip
  • Leverage the trillion-dollar chip industry's fabrication tools
  • Can operate at ~1 kelvin ("hot" qubits), easing cryogenics
❌ Cons
  • Qubit-to-qubit variability
  • Still at small scale (around a dozen qubits per device)
  • Crosstalk and wiring at density

Who: Intel (Tunnel Falls, 12 qubits, 2023), Diraq, Quantum Motion, Silicon Quantum Computing (phosphorus atoms in silicon), Equal1, and others.

21.7Topological qubits

The idea: store information not in any single particle but in the global, "topological" properties of exotic quasiparticles called non-abelian anyons, such as Majorana zero modes. Local noise can't easily disturb global properties, so the qubits would be intrinsically protected.

💡 Lightbulb moment

Analogy: a knot in a rope. You can wiggle and shake the rope (local noise), but the knot stays a knot. You'd have to do something drastic and global to undo it.

Status: this is Microsoft's long-term bet, using semiconductor–superconductor nanowires. The road has been bumpy: a 2018 Nature paper was retracted in 2021. In February 2025 Microsoft announced Majorana 1, a chip it described as built on topological qubits, alongside a Nature paper on measurements in these devices. Many physicists remain sceptical that topological qubits have been conclusively demonstrated, and the paper's editorial notes said it did not prove the presence of Majorana modes. DARPA has included Microsoft in its program evaluating paths to utility-scale quantum computers. Topological qubits remain unproven but potentially transformative.

21.10Modular quantum computers

No single chip or trap is likely to hold millions of qubits. Every major roadmap therefore relies on modularity: linking many smaller processors with microwave couplers, photonic links, or ion shuttling. In 2025, Oxford researchers ran a distributed quantum algorithm across two separate ion-trap modules connected by a photonic link. Linking modules is to quantum computing what networking was to classical supercomputing.

⚖️ Platform shoot-out (summary)

👍 The good
  • Superconducting: fastest gates and proven below-threshold error correction
  • Trapped ions: the highest fidelities and all-to-all connectivity
  • Neutral atoms: thousands of identical, movable qubits
👎 The tricky
  • Superconducting: extreme cryogenics and a wiring bottleneck
  • Trapped ions: slow gates and hard to scale beyond a few hundred per trap
  • Photonic, spin and topological: key challenges still unproven at scale

🎒 Pack these 3 ideas

  1. DiVincenzo's criteria: scalable, initialisable, coherent, universal, measurable (plus two for communication).
  2. Compare error rates and logical-qubit performance, not just qubit counts.
  3. Every roadmap relies on modularity: linking many smaller processors.
