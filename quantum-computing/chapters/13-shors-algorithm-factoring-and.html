RSA encryption is like a padlock that anyone can snap shut (multiplying two primes is easy) but only the owner can open (splitting the product back into primes is hard). Shor found a back door: factoring can be turned into finding the rhythm (period) of a repeating sequence, and quantum computers are brilliant at finding rhythms. Today's machines are far too small and noisy to do this to real keys, but the clock is ticking.

13.1Why the world cares

In 1994, Peter Shor discovered a quantum algorithm that factors integers and computes discrete logarithms in polynomial time. The security of RSA, Diffie–Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography, which protect almost all internet traffic, digital signatures and cryptocurrency wallets, rests on these problems being hard. Shor's algorithm turned quantum computing from a curiosity into a strategic priority.

13.2RSA in a nutshell (toy example)

  1. Choose two primes: p = 5, q = 11. Public modulus N = 55.
  2. Compute φ = (p − 1)(q − 1) = 40.
  3. Choose a public exponent e = 3 (it shares no factor with 40).
  4. The private key is d = 27, since 3 × 27 = 81 = 2 × 40 + 1.
  5. Encrypt message m = 7: c = 7³ mod 55 = 343 mod 55 = 13.
  6. Decrypt: 13²⁷ mod 55 = 7 ✓.

Anyone can see N and e. To find d, you need φ, which requires factoring N. For real keys, N has 617 decimal digits.

13.3The trick: factoring reduces to period finding

  1. Pick a random number a less than N (if gcd(a, N) ≠ 1, you've already found a factor).
  2. Find the period r of the sequence a¹, a², a³, … (mod N), meaning the smallest r > 0 with aʳ ≡ 1 (mod N). This is the hard step, and the only part done on a quantum computer.
  3. If r is even and a^(r/2) ≢ −1 (mod N), then gcd(a^(r/2) − 1, N) and gcd(a^(r/2) + 1, N) are factors of N.
  4. Otherwise, pick a new a and try again. (This works for at least half of all choices of a.)

The period is r = 4 (even ✓). Then a^(r/2) = 7² = 49, and 49 mod 15 = 4, which isn't −1 (that would be 14) ✓.

  • gcd(49 − 1, 15) = gcd(48, 15) = 3
  • gcd(49 + 1, 15) = gcd(50, 15) = 5

15 = 3 × 5 ✓

🧮 Pencil & paper

One more: factoring 21 with a = 2. Powers mod 21: 2, 4, 8, 16, 11, 1, so r = 6. Then 2³ = 8, gcd(7, 21) = 7 and gcd(9, 21) = 3. 21 = 3 × 7 ✓

For small numbers we can find r by listing powers. For a 2,048-bit N, the period can be astronomically large, and listing powers would take longer than the age of the universe. That's where the quantum computer comes in.

13.5The quantum part: finding the period

✏️ Show the original text sketch
Register A (t ≈ 2n qubits): |0…0⟩ ─[H⊗…⊗H]───●─────────[QFT⁻¹]─── M → measure k
                                              │
Register B (n qubits):      |0…1⟩ ───────────[ × aˣ mod N ]─────── (optionally measure)
  1. Put register A into a superposition of all x from 0 to 2ᵗ − 1.
  2. Compute aˣ mod N into register B, giving Σ |x⟩|aˣ mod N⟩. (This modular exponentiation is by far the most expensive part, using millions or billions of gates for real key sizes.)
  3. Measuring register B (or just ignoring it) leaves register A in a superposition of x values that all share the same aˣ mod N. These x values are spaced exactly r apart, so it's a periodic state.
  4. Apply the inverse QFT. Interference concentrates the amplitude on values k ≈ j · 2ᵗ/r for integers j.
  5. Measure k. Use the continued fractions algorithm (classical) on k/2ᵗ to recover r.
🧮 Pencil & paper

For N = 15, a = 7 (r = 4), with t = 4 qubits (2ᵗ = 16): the measurement gives k = 0, 4, 8, or 12, each with 25% probability.

These estimates assume physical error rates around 0.1% and full error correction (Chapter 20). The steady drop in estimates over the years is one reason security agencies urge migrating now (Chapter 25).

13.7What's actually been demonstrated

  • 2001: IBM factored 15 using a 7-qubit NMR (nuclear magnetic resonance) device.
  • 2012: 21 was factored on photonic and superconducting hardware.
  • Caveat: these demonstrations used "compiled" circuits that relied on knowing the answer in advance. A 2013 paper pointedly titled "Pretending to factor large numbers on a quantum computer" showed how misleading such demos can be.
  • No genuine run of Shor's algorithm has factored anything cryptographically meaningful as of late 2026. Be sceptical of headlines claiming "quantum computer breaks RSA". These typically use small numbers, hybrid tricks that don't scale, or annealers.

13.8Generalisations and relatives

  • Discrete logarithms: Shor's algorithm also solves gˣ = h for x, breaking Diffie–Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography.
  • The hidden subgroup problem (HSP): Simon's, Shor's and several other algorithms are all special cases of finding a hidden subgroup of an abelian (commutative) group, which quantum computers do efficiently. For some non-abelian groups, no efficient quantum algorithm is known. Two of these cases relate to graph isomorphism and to lattice problems, and that gap is one reason lattice-based cryptography is believed to be quantum-safe.
  • Regev's algorithm (2023): a new factoring algorithm that needs fewer gates per run but more runs and more qubits. It's an active research area for optimising quantum cryptanalysis.

⚖️ Shor's algorithm

👍 The good
  • Super-polynomial speedup for factoring and discrete logarithms
  • Only the period-finding step is quantum; the rest is classical
  • Resource estimates keep falling (about 20 million qubits in 2019, under 1 million in 2025)
👎 The tricky
  • Needs a large fault-tolerant machine that doesn't exist yet
  • Modular exponentiation takes millions to billions of gates
  • Early 'factoring demos' relied on knowing the answer in advance

🎒 Pack these 3 ideas

  1. Factoring reduces to period finding: the smallest r with aʳ ≡ 1 (mod N).
  2. Factors come from gcd(a^(r/2) ± 1, N): with a = 7 and r = 4, 15 = 3 × 5.
  3. The 2025 estimate for RSA-2048: under 1 million noisy qubits, under a week.
