The math of quantum mechanics works perfectly, but what does it mean? Does a measurement really 'collapse' reality? Does every outcome happen in a different world? Are particles guided by a hidden wave? Nobody knows for sure. The good news: your quantum computer works the same whichever story you believe.

31.1The measurement problem

Quantum mechanics has two rules: smooth, reversible evolution (Schrödinger's equation) and abrupt, random collapse on measurement. But what counts as a measurement? Where's the line between the quantum system and the classical observer? Physicists still disagree. This is the measurement problem, and different interpretations answer it differently.

31.2The main interpretations

InterpretationCore ideaDoes collapse really happen?
Copenhagen (Bohr, Heisenberg)The wavefunction is a tool for predicting measurement results; don't ask what happens between measurementsYes, as an update on measurement
Many-worlds (Everett, 1957)The wavefunction is all there is; every possible outcome happens, in branching "worlds"No; the universe branches
Pilot wave / de Broglie–BohmParticles always have definite positions, guided by a real wave; non-local hidden variablesNo; outcomes are determined but unknowable
Objective collapse (GRW, Penrose)Collapse is a real physical process that happens spontaneously, especially for big objectsYes; physically real, and in principle testable
QBismThe wavefunction represents an agent's personal beliefs about future experiencesIt's just belief updating
Relational QM (Rovelli)States are relative to observers; there's no observer-independent stateRelative to each observer
Consistent historiesAssign probabilities to coherent sequences of eventsNo collapse needed

31.3Does it matter for quantum computing?

Not for practice. All standard interpretations make the same predictions for every experiment we can do today, so a quantum computer works the same way whichever you believe. Philosophically, David Deutsch argues that Shor's algorithm supports many-worlds ("where else was the number factored?"). Others reply that the computation is simply interference of amplitudes, with no need for many worlds.

31.4Decoherence: a partial answer

Decoherence explains why we never see superpositions of everyday objects: interaction with the environment rapidly spreads quantum information outward, making different outcomes effectively unable to interfere. It explains why the world looks classical, but not, on its own, why we see one particular outcome.

31.5What Bell tests settled

Bell-test experiments (Nobel Prize 2022) rule out local realism: you can't have both (1) pre-existing definite values for all measurements and (2) no influence faster than light. Every interpretation must give up something. Many-worlds gives up single outcomes, pilot-wave gives up locality, and Copenhagen and QBism give up a certain kind of realism.

⚖️ Arguing about interpretations

👍 Why it's worth thinking about
  • Sharpens what 'measurement' really means
  • Inspires real experiments (objective collapse is testable in principle)
  • Deutsch's many-worlds reading of Shor's algorithm makes a great debate
👎 Why you can relax
  • They all make the same predictions for today's experiments
  • Decoherence explains why the world looks classical, but not which outcome we see
  • Your circuits behave identically under every interpretation

🎒 Pack these 3 ideas

  1. The measurement problem: what counts as a measurement?
  2. All standard interpretations predict the same experimental results.
  3. Bell tests rule out local realism, so every interpretation gives something up.
