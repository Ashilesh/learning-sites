⚡ In 30 seconds (no jargon, promise)

Gates are moves you make on the qubit's arrow. X flips it upside down (0 ↔ 1). Z spins it around, flipping the hidden phase. The Hadamard (H) is the superstar: it turns a sure 0 into a perfect 50/50 blend. Do H twice and, surprise, the randomness cancels and you get 0 again with certainty. That cancellation is interference, the trick behind every quantum algorithm.

6.1What a gate does

A quantum gate is an operation that changes a qubit's state. Mathematically it's a unitary matrix. Geometrically, a single-qubit gate is a rotation of the Bloch sphere. Unlike most classical gates, every quantum gate is reversible.

6.2The X gate (bit flip, the "quantum NOT")

X = [0 1]      X|0⟩ = |1⟩      X|1⟩ = |0⟩
    [1 0]

Rotates the Bloch sphere 180° around the x-axis. Acting on a superposition, it swaps the amplitudes: X(α|0⟩ + β|1⟩) = β|0⟩ + α|1⟩.

🧮 Pencil & paper

Example: X|+⟩ = |+⟩, because swapping two equal amplitudes changes nothing. |+⟩ is an eigenstate of X.

🤓 Did you know?

The T gate is also called the 'π/8 gate', even though it rotates by π/4. Historical names are weird.

6.3The Z gate (phase flip)

Z = [1  0]      Z|0⟩ = |0⟩      Z|1⟩ = −|1⟩
    [0 −1]

Rotates 180° around the z-axis. It does nothing to |0⟩ and |1⟩ individually (well, −|1⟩ is the same as |1⟩ up to global phase) but flips the relative phase of superpositions:

🧮 Pencil & paper

Example: Z|+⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2 = |−⟩. Measuring before and after in the Z basis shows no difference (50/50 both times), but the states are now orthogonal. This is a change that only interference can reveal.

6.4The Y gate

Y = [0 −i]      Y|0⟩ = i|1⟩      Y|1⟩ = −i|0⟩
    [i  0]

Rotates 180° around the y-axis. It's both a bit flip and a phase flip (Y = iXZ). X, Y and Z together are the Pauli gates, the building blocks of error descriptions and error correction.

6.5The Hadamard gate (H): the superposition maker

H = 1/√2 · [1  1]
           [1 −1]
InputOutput
|0⟩|+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2
|1⟩|−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2
|+⟩|0⟩
|−⟩|1⟩

H swaps the Z basis and the X basis. Applying it twice gets you back where you started: HH = I.

🧮 Pencil & paper

Worked example: the most important calculation in this guide.

Start with |0⟩ and apply H twice.

Step 1: H|0⟩ = (1/√2)|0⟩ + (1/√2)|1⟩. If we measured now: 50/50.

Step 2: Apply H again, using H|0⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 and H|1⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2:

H(1/√2|0⟩ + 1/√2|1⟩) = 1/√2 · H|0⟩            + 1/√2 · H|1⟩
                     = 1/√2 · (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2   + 1/√2 · (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2
                     = ½|0⟩ + ½|1⟩             + ½|0⟩ − ½|1⟩
                     = (½ + ½)|0⟩  +  (½ − ½)|1⟩
                     =     1·|0⟩   +      0·|1⟩

Result: |0⟩ with 100% certainty.

Why this is astonishing. Compare with a classical random process: flip a fair coin (50/50), then flip it again. You still get 50/50, because randomness piled on randomness stays random. But two "quantum coin flips" return a definite answer. The two paths leading to |1⟩ had amplitudes +½ and −½, and they cancelled. The two paths leading to |0⟩ had amplitudes +½ and +½, and they reinforced.

Path (after 1st H → after 2nd H)    Amplitude
|0⟩ → |0⟩ → |0⟩                     (1/√2)(+1/√2) = +½  ┐
|0⟩ → |1⟩ → |0⟩                     (1/√2)(+1/√2) = +½  ┘ → |0⟩ total = +1   ✔ constructive
|0⟩ → |0⟩ → |1⟩                     (1/√2)(+1/√2) = +½  ┐
|0⟩ → |1⟩ → |1⟩                     (1/√2)(−1/√2) = −½  ┘ → |1⟩ total =  0   ✘ destructive

Every quantum algorithm is an elaborate version of this: choreograph the paths so wrong answers cancel and right answers add up.

6.6Phase gates: S, T and P(φ)

These leave |0⟩ alone and multiply |1⟩ by a phase:

P(φ) = [1    0    ]      S = P(π/2) = [1 0]      T = P(π/4) = [1     0     ]
       [0  e^(iφ) ]                   [0 i]                   [0  e^(iπ/4) ]
  • S is a quarter turn around the z-axis (S² = Z).
  • T is an eighth turn (T² = S). Historically it's also called the "π/8 gate".
🧮 Pencil & paper

Example: S|+⟩ = (|0⟩ + i|1⟩)/√2 = |+i⟩, a quarter turn around the equator.

The T gate is special. Together with H and CNOT it makes a universal gate set, but it's also the most expensive gate to perform in error-corrected machines (Chapter 20).

6.7Rotation gates: Rx, Ry, Rz

For any angle θ, you can rotate around any axis:

Rx(θ) = [ cos(θ/2)   −i·sin(θ/2) ]       Ry(θ) = [ cos(θ/2)  −sin(θ/2) ]
        [ −i·sin(θ/2)   cos(θ/2) ]               [ sin(θ/2)   cos(θ/2) ]

Rz(θ) = [ e^(−iθ/2)      0     ]
        [    0       e^(iθ/2)  ]
🧮 Pencil & paper

Worked example: make a qubit that gives 0 with 75% probability.
Ry(θ)|0⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + sin(θ/2)|1⟩. We want cos²(θ/2) = 0.75, so cos(θ/2) = √3/2, so θ/2 = 30° and θ = 60° = π/3.
Result: Ry(π/3)|0⟩ = (√3/2)|0⟩ + (1/2)|1⟩. ✓

Rotation gates with adjustable angles are the "knobs" in variational algorithms and quantum machine learning (Chapter 16).

6.8Any single-qubit gate is three rotations

Every single-qubit unitary can be written (up to global phase) as Rz(α) · Ry(β) · Rz(γ), much like how any orientation of an object in 3-D can be reached with three rotations (Euler angles). Qiskit's general single-qubit gate U(θ, φ, λ) packages this.

⚖️ Single-qubit gates

👍 The good
  • Simple rotations, easy to picture on the Bloch sphere
  • Any single-qubit gate is three rotations (like Euler angles)
  • Rotation angles are tunable 'knobs' for variational algorithms
👎 The tricky
  • On their own they can't create entanglement
  • Small over- or under-rotations pile up as coherent errors
  • The T gate is expensive to do fault-tolerantly

🎒 Pack these 3 ideas

  1. Single-qubit gates are rotations of the Bloch sphere.
  2. HH = I: two 'quantum coin flips' give a certain result because the paths to |1⟩ cancel.
  3. T, together with H and CNOT, makes a universal gate set, and it's the priciest gate on error-corrected machines.

🧠 Check yourself +10 XP per first-try answer

1. What do you get from H applied to |0⟩ twice?

The two paths to |1⟩ have amplitudes +½ and −½, and they cancel.

2. What does Z do to |+⟩?

Z flips the relative phase: (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2 = |−⟩.

3. Which angle θ makes Ry(θ)|0⟩ give 0 with 75% probability?

cos²(θ/2) = 0.75, so θ/2 = 30° and θ = 60°.
