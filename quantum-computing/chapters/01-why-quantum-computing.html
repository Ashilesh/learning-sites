Normal computers think in bits: tiny switches that are either OFF (0) or ON (1). A quantum computer uses qubits, which follow the rules of atoms and light. They can blend 0 and 1, link up with each other in spooky ways, and their possibilities can cancel out like waves. That doesn't make them faster at everything. It makes them a different kind of tool: amazing for a few special problems (designing molecules, cracking certain codes) and useless for Netflix.

1.1The one-paragraph answer

A quantum computer is a machine that stores and processes information using the rules of quantum physics, the rules that govern atoms, electrons and light. Ordinary computers use bits that are always either 0 or 1. Quantum computers use qubits, which can be in a blend of 0 and 1 (superposition), can be linked to each other in ways that have no everyday equivalent (entanglement), and whose possibilities can reinforce or cancel each other like waves (interference). For certain problems, including simulating molecules, factoring huge numbers and some kinds of search, this lets a quantum computer reach an answer in far fewer steps than any known method on an ordinary computer. For most everyday tasks like email, games and spreadsheets, it offers no advantage at all.

1.2Where ordinary computers hit a wall

Example 1: Simulating nature. Suppose you want to simulate a group of tiny quantum particles, each of which can be in one of two states (like a tiny magnet pointing up or down). Because of superposition and entanglement, an exact description of n such particles requires keeping track of 2ⁿ numbers at once. Each number takes about 16 bytes of memory.

Particles (n)Numbers needed (2ⁿ)Memory neededComparable to
101,02416 kilobytesA small text file
30~1 billion16 gigabytesA good laptop
50~1 quadrillion16 petabytesThe biggest supercomputers
80~10²⁴~16 yottabytesMore than all storage on Earth
300~10⁹⁰ImpossibleMore numbers than atoms in the observable universe (about 10⁸⁰)

Every time you add one particle, the memory doubles. This is why chemists can't exactly simulate even moderately sized molecules, and why physicist Richard Feynman argued in 1981 that if nature is quantum, the natural way to simulate it is with a computer that is itself quantum. A quantum computer with 300 qubits naturally holds a state that would need 10⁹⁰ numbers to write down.

Example 2: Factoring. Much of today's internet security (RSA encryption) relies on the fact that multiplying two large prime numbers is easy, but splitting the result back into its primes is extraordinarily hard.

  • Multiplying: 3,571 × 4,253 = 15,187,463. Any calculator does this instantly.
  • Factoring: given 15,187,463, find the two primes. This is much harder, and it gets exponentially harder as numbers grow.

The largest RSA challenge number ever factored classically (RSA-250, 829 bits) took about 2,700 CPU-core-years in 2020. Real RSA keys are 2,048 bits, and are considered far out of reach classically. In 1994, Peter Shor showed that a large enough quantum computer could factor such numbers efficiently. A 2025 estimate by Craig Gidney (Google) put the requirement at under one million noisy qubits running for less than a week. That machine doesn't exist yet, but it's why governments are already replacing encryption (Chapter 25).

1.3What a quantum computer is NOT

🚫 Myth alert

Misconception: "A quantum computer is just a much faster computer."
It isn't faster at ordinary things. Its individual operations are actually slower than a modern CPU's. Its power comes from a different kind of computation that only helps for problems with special mathematical structure.

💡 Lightbulb moment

Analogy: A laser isn't a "better light bulb". You'd never light your living room with one. But a laser can do things a light bulb never could, like cutting steel or reading a Blu-ray disc. Quantum computers are like that: a specialised tool, not an upgrade to your laptop.

🚫 Myth alert

Misconception: "It tries every possible answer at the same time and picks the right one."
This is the most common myth. A qubit register can be in a superposition of many possibilities, but when you look at it (measure it), you get just one random result. If that were all, it would be useless. The real trick is interference: carefully designing the computation so that wrong answers cancel out and right answers reinforce, before you measure. Only some problems have enough structure to allow this. (Chapter 10 explains this in detail.)

1.4The three key ingredients

IngredientPlain-language meaningWhy it matters
SuperpositionA qubit can be in a combination of 0 and 1Lets a computer work with many possibilities in one state
EntanglementQubits can share a joint state that can't be described one-by-oneCreates correlations no classical system can have; required for any big speedup
InterferencePossibilities have "amplitudes" that can add up or cancelThe mechanism that steers the computation toward the right answer

Plus one unavoidable rule: measurement turns the quantum state into an ordinary classical result, randomly, and destroys the superposition in the process.

⚖️ Quantum computers at a glance

👍 The good
  • Naturally hold states that would need 10⁹⁰ numbers to write down classically
  • Exponential speedups expected for factoring and for simulating molecules
  • Can do anything a classical computer can do (they are a strict generalisation)
👎 The tricky
  • Each operation is slower than a modern CPU's
  • No advantage for email, games or spreadsheets
  • Machines big enough to break RSA don't exist yet (late 2026)

🎒 Pack these 3 ideas

  1. Qubits use superposition, entanglement and interference. They do not 'try every answer at once'.
  2. Simulating n quantum particles exactly needs 2ⁿ numbers. At 300 particles that beats the number of atoms in the universe.
  3. Quantum computers are specialist co-processors, like a laser next to a light bulb.

🧠 Check yourself +10 XP per first-try answer

1. What is the real secret sauce of quantum algorithms?

Measuring gives one random result, so the trick is to steer amplitudes so that wrong answers cancel before you look.

2. Roughly how much memory does it take to store the exact state of 50 quantum particles?

2⁵⁰ ≈ 10¹⁵ numbers × 16 bytes ≈ 16 PB.

3. Which of these does Shor's algorithm threaten?

Shor breaks public-key schemes like RSA and elliptic curves. AES-256 stays safe.
