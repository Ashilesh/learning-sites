⚡ In 30 seconds (no jargon, promise)

A quantum circuit is like sheet music: each line is a qubit, time flows left to right, and the boxes are gates. You start every qubit at 0, play the gates, then measure to get a string of bits. Because each run gives a random answer, you run it hundreds or thousands of times ('shots') and look at the histogram.

8.1Reading a circuit diagram

Quantum programs are usually drawn as circuits:

✏️ Show the original text sketch
q0: |0⟩ ──H────●────M══╗
               │       ║
q1: |0⟩ ───────X────M══╬═╗
                       ║ ║
c:  ═══════════════════╩═╩═   (classical bits)
  • Each horizontal line is a qubit, flowing left to right in time.
  • Boxes (H, X, …) are gates.
  • ● connected to X (often drawn ⊕) is a CNOT: ● marks the control.
  • M (often drawn as a meter) is a measurement.
  • Double lines carry classical bits.

8.2The life cycle of a quantum program

  1. Initialise: all qubits start in |0⟩.
  2. Apply gates: the circuit transforms the state.
  3. Measure: get a classical bit string.
  4. Repeat: run the whole thing many times (each run is a "shot") to build up a histogram of results.
🧮 Pencil & paper

Example: the Bell circuit above, run 1,000 times on an ideal simulator:

  01        0
  10        0
  11      493    ████████████████████████

On real hardware you'd see a few percent of 01 and 10 due to errors.

8.3Why we need many shots

Each shot gives one random sample. To estimate a probability p from N shots, the statistical uncertainty is about √(p(1−p)/N).

🧮 Pencil & paper

Example: Estimating a 50% probability with 1,000 shots gives ±1.6%. With 100,000 shots you'd get ±0.16%. Ten times the precision costs a hundred times the shots. This "shot noise" is a real cost in algorithms like VQE that need precise averages.

8.4Circuit size: width, depth and gate count

  • Width: number of qubits.
  • Depth: number of time steps (layers of gates that can run in parallel).
  • Gate count, and especially the two-qubit gate count, matters because two-qubit gates are the noisiest.

Depth matters enormously on today's machines. Qubits decohere over time, so a circuit must finish before the quantum information fades.

8.5Mid-circuit measurement and feed-forward

Modern hardware can measure some qubits in the middle of a circuit and use the result to decide which gates to apply next (classical feed-forward). Circuits that do this are called dynamic circuits. They're essential for teleportation (Chapter 9) and error correction (Chapter 20).

8.6Useful principles

  • Deferred measurement: any measurement in the middle of a circuit can be moved to the end, replacing classical control with quantum-controlled gates. Useful for analysing circuits on paper.
  • Implicit measurement: qubits you never measure at the end can be treated as if they'd been measured, without changing the statistics of the others.

8.7Circuit identities worth knowing

IdentityMeaning
X² = Y² = Z² = H² = IThese gates are their own inverses
HXH = Z and HZH = XH swaps the roles of X and Z
SWAP = CNOT₁₂ · CNOT₂₁ · CNOT₁₂Swap from three CNOTs
(H⊗H) · CNOT₁₂ · (H⊗H) = CNOT₂₁Hadamards reverse a CNOT's direction
CZ = (I⊗H) · CNOT · (I⊗H)Convert between CZ and CNOT
🧮 Pencil & paper

Example: verify HZH = X on |0⟩. H|0⟩ = |+⟩, then Z|+⟩ = |−⟩, then H|−⟩ = |1⟩. And X|0⟩ = |1⟩ ✓.

8.8Uncomputation: cleaning up after yourself

Quantum algorithms often use extra helper qubits (ancillas) for scratch work. Leftover "garbage" in ancillas can become entangled with the answer and ruin interference. The fix: after using a result, run the steps that produced it in reverse to return the ancillas to |0⟩. This is called uncomputation, and it's possible because every quantum gate is reversible.

8.9Worked example: a three-qubit GHZ circuit, step by step🧮 worked example

✏️ Show the original text sketch
q0: |0⟩ ──H──●───────
             │
q1: |0⟩ ─────X───●───
                 │
q2: |0⟩ ─────────X───
StepState (written as q0 q1 q2)
Start|000⟩
H on q0(|000⟩ + |100⟩)/√2
CNOT q0 → q1(|000⟩ + |110⟩)/√2
CNOT q1 → q2(|000⟩ + |111⟩)/√2 ← GHZ state

Measuring gives 000 or 111, 50% each. Scaling this pattern up, record-sized GHZ states (with dozens to over a hundred qubits) are now used as hardware benchmarks.

8.10Other models of quantum computation

The circuit model is the most common, but there are others. All are equivalent in power (each can simulate the others with only polynomial overhead):

ModelIdeaWhere it's used
Circuit modelSequence of gates, then measurementMost hardware and software today
Measurement-based (one-way)Prepare a big entangled "cluster state", then compute only by measuring qubits one by oneNatural fit for photonic quantum computers (PsiQuantum's "fusion-based" approach is a variant)
AdiabaticEncode the answer in the lowest-energy state of a Hamiltonian; slowly transform from an easy Hamiltonian to the problem oneInspiration for quantum annealing (D-Wave)
TopologicalBraid exotic quasiparticles ("anyons") around each other; the braid pattern is the computationMicrosoft's long-term approach; naturally resistant to errors
Quantum Turing machineQuantum version of a Turing machineTheory (Deutsch 1985)

⚖️ The circuit model

👍 The good
  • Simple, visual, and standard across hardware and software
  • Dynamic circuits allow mid-circuit measurement and feed-forward
  • As powerful as every other model of quantum computation
👎 The tricky
  • Each run gives one sample, so you need many shots
  • Deep circuits die from decoherence on today's hardware
  • Leftover 'garbage' in ancillas ruins interference unless you uncompute

🎒 Pack these 3 ideas

  1. Life cycle: initialise, apply gates, measure, repeat (shots).
  2. Shot noise ≈ √(p(1−p)/N): ten times the precision costs a hundred times the shots.
  3. Depth and two-qubit gate count decide whether a circuit survives the noise.
