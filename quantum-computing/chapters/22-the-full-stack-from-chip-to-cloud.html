A quantum computer is a layer cake. On top: your application and the code you write. In the middle: a compiler that translates your gates into what the chip can actually do, plus error correction. At the bottom: control electronics firing precise pulses at qubits kept in an ultra-cold fridge or a vacuum chamber. And you can use real ones over the cloud today.

22.2Cryogenics

Superconducting (and spin) qubits live inside dilution refrigerators, which reach about 10 millikelvin, roughly 270 times colder than outer space (2.7 K). These are the "golden chandeliers" in quantum computing photos: stacked plates at progressively colder temperatures, with cables winding down to the chip at the bottom. A dilution refrigerator works by mixing two isotopes of helium (helium-3 and helium-4). The growing challenge is wiring: every qubit needs control lines, and bringing thousands of cables into a fridge is impractical. Solutions include cryogenic control chips (cryo-CMOS), multiplexing, and larger custom fridges (IBM, Google, Bluefors and others).

22.3Control electronics

Each gate is a precisely shaped pulse:

  • Superconducting qubits: nanosecond-scale microwave pulses from arbitrary waveform generators.
  • Ions and atoms: carefully timed laser pulses, or microwave and magnetic-field-gradient pulses.
  • Fast feedback: FPGAs and custom chips read measurement results and decide the next operation within microseconds, which is essential for error correction and dynamic circuits.

Systems need continual calibration because qubit properties drift over hours.

22.4Compilation (transpilation)

Your program is written with abstract gates on abstract qubits. The compiler must:

  1. Decompose gates into the hardware's native gates. For example, many superconducting systems natively support √X, Rz, and one two-qubit gate (CZ or ECR). Rz is often "virtual": done for free by adjusting the reference frame of later pulses.
  2. Map logical qubits to physical qubits, choosing the best-quality ones.
  3. Route: insert SWAP gates when two qubits that need to interact aren't physically connected.
  4. Optimise: cancel redundant gates, merge rotations.
  5. Schedule operations in time.
🧮 Pencil & paper

Worked example: routing. On a chip where qubits are connected in a line q0 — q1 — q2, a CNOT between q0 and q2 isn't directly possible. The compiler swaps q1 and q2 (3 CNOTs), does the CNOT between neighbours, and may swap back. One logical CNOT becomes 4 to 7 physical CNOTs, which is why connectivity matters so much.

22.5Accessing quantum computers via the cloud

You don't need to own a quantum computer. As of 2026, you can access real hardware through:

  • IBM Quantum Platform (superconducting; includes a free tier with limited monthly runtime)
  • Amazon Braket (multiple vendors: IonQ, Rigetti, IQM, QuEra and others)
  • Microsoft Azure Quantum (multiple vendors)
  • Google Cloud / Google Quantum AI (mainly for research partners)
  • Vendor platforms such as Quantinuum Nexus and IonQ Cloud

Free tiers and pricing change often, so check each provider's site.

22.6Quantum + supercomputers

Quantum processors are increasingly installed alongside supercomputers: at RIKEN in Japan, Jülich in Germany, Oak Ridge in the USA, and elsewhere. They act as accelerators in hybrid workflows. IBM calls this "quantum-centric supercomputing", and NVIDIA's CUDA-Q platform targets programming such hybrid systems.

⚖️ The full stack

👍 The good
  • Cloud access: real hardware without owning a fridge
  • Compilers hide the hardware details
  • Quantum-plus-supercomputer hybrids are emerging
👎 The tricky
  • Wiring thousands of qubits into a fridge is impractical
  • Limited connectivity: one logical CNOT can become 4–7 physical CNOTs
  • Constant recalibration, because qubit properties drift

🎒 Pack these 3 ideas

  1. Dilution refrigerators reach about 10 mK, roughly 270× colder than outer space.
  2. Transpilers decompose, map, route (insert SWAPs), optimise and schedule.
  3. State-vector simulators reach about 30 qubits on a laptop and 40–50 on a supercomputer.
