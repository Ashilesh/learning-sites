19.1Why qubits are so fragile

💡 Lightbulb moment

Analogy: Keeping a qubit coherent is like keeping a soap bubble intact while juggling it. You need to touch it (to compute), but every touch, breeze or speck of dust can pop it.

A qubit must be isolated from its environment (so it doesn't decohere) yet controllable (so we can apply gates and measure). These two demands pull in opposite directions, and that tension defines quantum engineering.

19.3T₁ and T₂: the qubit's lifetime

  • T₁ (relaxation time): how long, on average, before an excited qubit |1⟩ decays to |0⟩.
  • T₂ (coherence time): how long a superposition keeps a well-defined phase. Always T₂ ≤ 2T₁.

Superconducting100 µs – 1 ms20 – 100 nsThousands

What matters isn't raw coherence time but the ratio of coherence time to gate time, and ultimately the error per gate.

19.4Why even tiny errors are a big problem

If each gate succeeds with probability F (fidelity), a circuit of G gates succeeds with roughly F^G.

🧮 Pencil & paper

Worked example: with 99.9% fidelity (0.1% error per gate):

GatesSuccess probability
1000.999¹⁰⁰ ≈ 90%
1,0000.999¹⁰⁰⁰ ≈ 37%
10,0000.999¹⁰⁰⁰⁰ ≈ 0.005%
1,000,000,000Effectively 0

Useful algorithms like Shor's or large chemistry simulations need billions to trillions of operations, so they require error rates of roughly 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻¹² per operation. No physical qubit will ever be that good. The best today are around 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁴. The gap must be closed by quantum error correction (Chapter 20).

19.5Where noise comes from

  • Heat: thermal energy excites qubits. This is why superconducting qubits run at ~10 millikelvin.
  • Material defects: microscopic "two-level systems" in surfaces and oxides absorb energy from superconducting qubits.
  • Electromagnetic noise: stray fields, imperfect shielding, noisy control lines.
  • Laser noise: intensity and phase fluctuations (ions and atoms).
  • Magnetic field fluctuations: dephase spin and atomic qubits.
  • Control imperfections: pulse distortion, calibration drift.
  • Cosmic rays and natural radioactivity: cause rare but correlated errors in superconducting chips. Some labs are exploring underground facilities and chip designs that block energy from spreading.

19.6How engineers fight back

  1. Better qubits: cleaner materials (e.g. tantalum instead of niobium for superconducting circuits), better designs (fluxonium, cat qubits), isotopically pure silicon.
  2. Isolation: cryogenics, ultra-high vacuum, magnetic shielding, vibration isolation.
  3. Better control: optimised pulse shapes (e.g. DRAG pulses), automated calibration, faster gates.
  4. Dynamical decoupling: periodically flipping idle qubits so slow noise cancels itself out (like the spin-echo technique in MRI).
  5. Error mitigation: statistical post-processing (Chapter 16.7).
  6. Error correction: the ultimate solution (Chapter 20).

⚖️ Fighting noise

👍 The good
  • Better materials (tantalum) and designs (fluxonium, cat qubits)
  • Dynamical decoupling cancels slow noise
  • The same fragility makes superb sensors (Chapter 27)
👎 The tricky
  • Errors compound exponentially with circuit size
  • Correlated errors (cosmic rays) hit many qubits at once
  • Isolation and control pull in opposite directions

🎒 Pack these 3 ideas

  1. T₁ = energy relaxation time, T₂ = phase coherence time (T₂ ≤ 2T₁).
  2. Success ≈ F^G: 0.999¹⁰⁰⁰ ≈ 37%.
  3. Useful algorithms need 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻¹² errors per operation; the best physical qubits today manage about 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁴.
