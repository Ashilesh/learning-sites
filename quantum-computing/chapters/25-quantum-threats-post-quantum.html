⚡ In 30 seconds (no jargon, promise)

Big quantum computers would break the public-key padlocks (RSA, elliptic curves) that protect the internet. There are two defences. Post-quantum cryptography uses new math problems believed to be hard even for quantum computers, and it runs on normal devices; it's already in your browser. Quantum key distribution uses photons so that any eavesdropper leaves fingerprints. Most experts say: migrate to post-quantum cryptography now.

25.3"Harvest now, decrypt later" and Mosca's inequality

Adversaries can record encrypted traffic today and decrypt it years from now, once a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) exists. So data that must stay secret for a long time is already at risk.

Mosca's inequality: let

  • x = how many years your data must stay secret,
  • y = how many years it will take you to migrate to quantum-safe cryptography,
  • z = how many years until a CRQC exists.

If x + y > z, you're already late.

🧮 Pencil & paper

Worked example: a hospital's patient records must stay confidential for 25 years (x = 25). Migrating all its systems will take 7 years (y = 7). If a CRQC arrives in 15 years (z = 15), then x + y = 32 > 15. Records encrypted today could be exposed for 17 years of their required secrecy period.

25.4The new standards

In August 2024, the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) published the first post-quantum standards after an eight-year global competition:

FN-DSA (FIPS 206, in preparation)NTRU latticesCompact digital signaturesFalcon
HQC (selected March 2025)Error-correcting codesBackup key encapsulationHQC

Migration timelines (examples):

  • USA: NIST's transition guidance proposes deprecating today's quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms after 2030 and disallowing them after 2035. The NSA's CNSA 2.0 requires national security systems to transition on a similar schedule, with some categories (like software signing) earlier.
  • UK: the NCSC's roadmap sets milestones for 2028 (discovery and planning), 2031 (priority migrations) and 2035 (completion).
  • EU: a coordinated roadmap (2025) asks member states to begin transitioning by the end of 2026 and protect high-risk systems by 2030.

Already deployed: hybrid key exchange combining classical X25519 with ML-KEM is now the default in major web browsers and at large CDNs such as Cloudflare, so a large share of web traffic is already post-quantum protected. Apple's iMessage (PQ3), Signal (PQXDH and later upgrades) and OpenSSH (ML-KEM hybrid key exchange by default since version 10.0) have also adopted post-quantum protection.

💡 Lightbulb moment

Why "hybrid"? The new algorithms are younger and less battle-tested. Combining a classical algorithm with a post-quantum one means an attacker must break both. (This caution was justified: SIKE, a post-quantum candidate, was broken in 2022 by a classical attack running on a laptop.)

25.5What organisations should do

  1. Inventory where public-key cryptography is used (TLS, VPNs, certificates, code signing, hardware tokens, IoT, databases).
  2. Prioritise long-lived secrets and long-lived systems (embedded devices last decades).
  3. Build crypto-agility: make algorithms swappable without redesigning systems.
  4. Deploy hybrid PQC where supported (TLS, SSH, VPNs).
  5. Ask vendors for their PQC roadmaps.
  6. Test performance: PQC keys and signatures are larger (an ML-KEM-768 public key is about 1.2 KB, versus 32 bytes for X25519).

25.6Cryptocurrencies

Bitcoin and Ethereum use elliptic-curve signatures (ECDSA, Schnorr), which Shor's algorithm breaks. Coins whose public keys are already visible on the blockchain are most exposed: early "pay-to-public-key" outputs, reused addresses, and some newer output types. Estimates of the vulnerable share of Bitcoin's supply are often around a quarter. Bitcoin mining (SHA-256 hashing) is only modestly affected by Grover. The communities are discussing post-quantum signature upgrades, but migrating a decentralised system is socially and technically difficult.

25.7Quantum Key Distribution: BB84

In 1984, Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard proposed the first quantum cryptography protocol, BB84. It lets Alice and Bob create a shared secret key, with any eavesdropping detectable by the laws of physics.

Ingredients: Alice sends single photons, each encoding one bit in one of two randomly chosen bases:

BasisBit 0Bit 1
+ (rectilinear)|0⟩ (horizontal)|1⟩ (vertical)
× (diagonal)|+⟩ (45°)|−⟩ (135°)

Bob measures each photon in a randomly chosen basis. If he picks the same basis as Alice, he gets her bit exactly. If he picks the other basis, he gets a random result (complementarity, Chapter 4.5).

  1. After sending, Alice and Bob publicly compare bases (never bits) and keep only the photons where they match. Their sifted key is 0011.
  2. They sacrifice a random sample of bits to check the error rate.

Catching an eavesdropper (Eve): Eve doesn't know Alice's bases. If she intercepts and measures each photon, she picks the wrong basis half the time and disturbs the photon. Bob then gets the wrong bit half of those times, so Eve introduces about 25% errors in the sifted key. Alice and Bob see the high error rate and abort. If errors are low enough (below about 11% for standard BB84), they apply error correction and privacy amplification to distil a shorter key that Eve knows essentially nothing about.

Other protocols:

  • E91 (Ekert, 1991): uses entangled pairs; security is linked to Bell-inequality violation.
  • MDI-QKD: removes vulnerabilities in the detectors.
  • Device-independent QKD: security doesn't even require trusting the devices (demonstrated in 2022 over short distances).
  • Twin-field QKD: reaches longer distances; experiments have exceeded 1,000 km of fibre.

Real-world QKD:

  • China's Micius satellite (launched 2016) performed satellite-to-ground QKD and enabled an intercontinental quantum-secured video call between Beijing and Vienna in 2017.
  • China operates a ~2,000 km Beijing–Shanghai QKD backbone using trusted relay nodes, and has expanded it into a larger national network.
  • Europe is building EuroQCI; ESA plans the Eagle-1 QKD satellite.

Limitations (why agencies prefer PQC):

  • QKD still needs an authenticated classical channel, which requires classical cryptography or pre-shared keys anyway.
  • Range is limited without trusted relay nodes, and each relay is a point of trust.
  • Real implementations have been hacked through hardware flaws (e.g. detector blinding attacks in 2010), not through the physics itself.
  • Expensive, point-to-point and hard to integrate.

25.8Quantum random number generators

Quantum measurement outcomes are fundamentally unpredictable, which makes them an ideal source of true randomness. QRNGs are already commercial products, even small chips in some smartphones. Certified randomness protocols go further, letting a user prove that numbers came from a quantum process (demonstrated by JPMorgan Chase and Quantinuum in 2025).

⚖️ Post-quantum cryptography vs. quantum key distribution

👍 The good
  • PQC runs on existing hardware and is already deployed (browsers, Signal, iMessage, OpenSSH)
  • QKD's security rests on physics rather than math assumptions
  • Quantum random number generators give true randomness
👎 The tricky
  • PQC keys are larger (an ML-KEM-768 public key is about 1.2 KB vs. 32 bytes for X25519)
  • QKD needs special hardware, an authenticated channel and trusted relays
  • Real QKD systems have been hacked through hardware flaws

🎒 Pack these 3 ideas

  1. Shor breaks RSA and ECC. AES-256 and SHA-256 survive.
  2. Mosca's inequality: if x + y > z, you're already late.
  3. NIST 2024 standards: ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA (plus FN-DSA and HQC). In BB84, Eve shows up as about 25% errors.
