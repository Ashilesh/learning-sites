The fix is the quantum repeater: build entanglement over short hops, store it, and stitch the hops together with entanglement swapping until the two ends are linked. Then teleport!

26.1What is a quantum internet?

A quantum internet would transmit qubits and entanglement between distant nodes, not just classical bits. It wouldn't replace the classical internet; it would add new capabilities:

  • Provably secure communication (QKD and beyond)
  • Linking quantum computers into larger distributed machines
  • Blind cloud quantum computing
  • Ultra-precise clock synchronisation and sensor networks
  • Very-long-baseline telescopes with dramatically better resolution

26.2The core problem: photon loss

Optical fibre loses about 0.2 dB per km at telecom wavelengths.

🧮 Pencil & paper

Worked example:

DistanceTotal lossFraction of photons arrivingWith a 10 GHz single-photon source
100 km20 dB1 in 100100 million per second
500 km100 dB1 in 10¹⁰~1 per second
1,000 km200 dB1 in 10²⁰~1 every 300 years

Classical networks solve loss with amplifiers that copy and boost the signal. Quantum signals can't be copied (no-cloning). The solution is the quantum repeater.

26.3Quantum repeaters

A quantum repeater chain breaks a long distance into short segments:

  1. Create entanglement across each short segment, where photon loss is manageable.
  2. Store it in a quantum memory until neighbouring segments are ready.
  3. Use entanglement swapping (Chapter 9.4) to join segments into longer-distance entanglement.
  4. Use entanglement purification to turn several noisy pairs into fewer, higher-quality pairs.

26.4Stages of development

A widely cited roadmap (Wehner, Elkouss and Hanson, Science, 2018) describes stages:

StageCapabilityStatus (late 2026)
0. Trusted repeater networksQKD between nodes that must be trustedDeployed (China, Europe, others)
1. Prepare-and-measureEnd-to-end QKD without trusted nodesDemonstrated over metropolitan distances
2. Entanglement distributionUsers share entangled pairsDemonstrated in testbeds
3. Quantum memory networksStore entanglement for later useEarly multi-node demonstrations
4. Few-qubit fault-tolerant nodesReliable local processingResearch
5. Quantum computing networksDistributed quantum computingLong-term goal

26.5Milestones

  • 2017: Micius satellite distributes entanglement over 1,200 km and teleports qubits from ground to orbit.
  • 2021: Delft (QuTech) operates the first three-node entanglement-based network with diamond NV centres.
  • 2024: Delft links nodes across ~25 km of deployed fibre between Delft and The Hague. Harvard and AWS entangle quantum memories across ~35 km of Boston-area fibre. USTC demonstrates multi-node metropolitan entanglement.
  • 2025: Oxford runs a distributed quantum algorithm across two linked ion-trap processors.
  • Testbeds: Chicago, New York/Long Island, Boston, the Netherlands, the EU (EuroQCI), China, and others.

⚖️ The quantum internet

👍 The good
  • Provably secure communication
  • Linking quantum computers into distributed machines
  • Better clocks, sensor networks and telescopes
👎 The tricky
  • Photon loss is brutal, and amplifiers aren't allowed
  • Quantum memories and repeaters are still early-stage
  • Fully fault-tolerant networks are a long-term goal

🎒 Pack these 3 ideas

  1. Fibre loses about 0.2 dB per km, so over 1,000 km only 1 photon in 10²⁰ arrives.
  2. Repeaters = short links + quantum memories + entanglement swapping + purification.
  3. Milestones: Micius over 1,200 km (2017), Delft's 3-node network (2021), metro fibre links of 25–35 km (2024).

🧠 Check yourself +10 XP per first-try answer

1. Why can't we amplify quantum signals the way classical ones are amplified?

Amplifying means copying, which quantum mechanics forbids.

2. Over 500 km of fibre (100 dB of loss), what fraction of photons arrive?

100 dB is a factor of 10¹⁰.

3. What joins short entangled links into long ones?

A Bell measurement at the middle node.
