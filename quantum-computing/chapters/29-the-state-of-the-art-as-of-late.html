29.1Where we are, in one paragraph

Quantum computing is in a transition from the NISQ era to the early fault-tolerant era. The best machines have hundreds to over a thousand physical qubits, with two-qubit error rates of about 0.1% or better on leading platforms. Error correction has crossed the critical "below threshold" line: bigger codes now produce better logical qubits. Experiments now run dozens of logical qubits at a time, up to around 70 in a 2026 report. Several beyond-classical demonstrations exist, increasingly with verifiable outputs. But there is still no widely accepted demonstration of a commercially useful quantum advantage, and no quantum computer can yet threaten real-world cryptography. Governments and industry are migrating to post-quantum cryptography anyway.

29.4What to watch next

  1. Logical qubit counts and logical error rates. Hundreds of logical qubits with error rates around 10⁻⁶ would be a turning point.
  2. Logical circuit depth. Can machines run millions of logical operations (the "megaquop" milestone)?
  3. Real-time decoding at scale.
  4. The first convincing useful advantage, most likely in physics, chemistry or materials simulation.
  5. Modular interconnects that link chips, traps and atom arrays.
  6. Independent validation of topological qubits.
  7. Falling resource estimates for Shor's algorithm, and the pace of PQC migration.

⚖️ Where the field stands

👍 The good
  • Below-threshold error correction achieved
  • Dozens of logical qubits (around 70 reported in 2026)
  • Large public and private investment worldwide
👎 The tricky
  • No widely accepted commercial advantage yet
  • Roadmaps are company claims and often slip
  • Topological qubits remain unvalidated

🎒 Pack these 3 ideas

  1. The field is moving from NISQ to early fault tolerance.
  2. Roadmaps: IBM Starling (2029), Quantinuum Apollo (by 2029), Google toward ~1 million physical qubits.
  3. Watch logical error rates, 'megaquop' depth, real-time decoding and modular links.
