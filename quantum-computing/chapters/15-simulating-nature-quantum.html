15.1Why this is the "killer app"

Chemistry, materials science and much of physics are governed by quantum mechanics. Electrons in a molecule are entangled with each other, and describing them exactly requires exponentially many numbers (Chapter 1.2). Classical methods like density functional theory (DFT) and coupled cluster use clever approximations and work remarkably well for many molecules. But they struggle with strongly correlated systems, where many electrons interact intensely. Those systems include:

  • Transition-metal catalysts (e.g. enzymes that fix nitrogen)
  • High-temperature superconductors
  • Some battery and magnetic materials
  • Excited states and chemical reaction dynamics

A quantum computer can represent these electrons natively, with roughly one qubit per electron orbital.

15.2Hamiltonian simulation: making qubits mimic a molecule

The goal is to implement e^(−iHt), the time evolution of a system with Hamiltonian H. Physical Hamiltonians are usually a sum of simple pieces, H = A + B + C + …, each easy to simulate on its own. The pieces don't commute, so we can't just apply them one after another, but we can alternate them in tiny time steps:

e^(−i(A+B)t)  ≈  ( e^(−iAt/n) · e^(−iBt/n) )ⁿ      (Trotter–Suzuki formula)
💡 Lightbulb moment

Analogy: You want to walk diagonally north-east across a city, but you can only walk along streets running north or east. Take many small alternating steps (east, north, east, north…) and you'll closely trace the diagonal. Smaller steps, better accuracy.

Seth Lloyd proved in 1996 that this approach efficiently simulates any "local" Hamiltonian, confirming Feynman's 1981 conjecture. Modern methods (linear combination of unitaries, qubitisation, quantum signal processing) achieve better precision with fewer gates.

15.3From molecules to qubits

  1. Choose a set of orbitals (a "basis set") for the electrons.
  2. Write the molecule's electronic Hamiltonian in terms of electrons hopping between orbitals and interacting.
  3. Map electrons to qubits using an encoding such as Jordan–Wigner or Bravyi–Kitaev, where each qubit says whether a spin-orbital is occupied (|1⟩) or empty (|0⟩).
  4. The Hamiltonian becomes a weighted sum of Pauli strings, such as 0.17·Z₀ + 0.12·Z₀Z₁ − 0.05·X₀X₁Y₂Y₃ + ….
🧮 Pencil & paper

Example: the hydrogen molecule (H₂) in the smallest standard basis has 4 spin-orbitals, so 4 qubits, which symmetry tricks can reduce to just 1 or 2. That's why H₂ is the "hello world" of quantum chemistry.

15.4Finding ground-state energies

The most important quantity is usually the ground-state energy, the lowest energy a molecule can have. Energy differences determine reaction rates, binding strengths and stability. The target precision is "chemical accuracy": about 1.6 millihartree (1 kcal/mol).

Resource estimates: early estimates for simulating FeMoco (Reiher et al., 2017) implied impractically long runtimes. Algorithmic improvements have since cut costs by many orders of magnitude, to roughly a few million physical qubits running for days (Lee et al., 2021). Further reductions continue.

15.6Analog quantum simulators

Instead of a general-purpose gate-based computer, you can build a quantum system that directly mimics another one: atoms trapped in a grid of laser light (an optical lattice) behaving like electrons in a crystal, or arrays of Rydberg atoms behaving like quantum magnets. These analog simulators are less flexible but can be very large. They've been used to observe exotic quantum phases, such as signatures of a topological spin liquid (Harvard and QuEra, 2021).

15.7An honest caveat

Two challenges are often under-appreciated:

  1. State preparation: QPE needs a starting state with decent overlap with the true ground state. For large, complex molecules, finding such a state may itself be hard.
  2. Classical methods keep improving. A 2023 study (Lee, Chan and collaborators) found no evidence of a generic exponential quantum advantage for ground-state chemistry, though large polynomial speedups for important hard cases remain plausible.

The consensus: quantum simulation is the most promising application, but useful advantage will likely need fault-tolerant machines and carefully chosen problems.

  • A natural fit: quantum hardware for quantum problems
  • Huge targets: fertiliser, drugs, batteries, superconductors
