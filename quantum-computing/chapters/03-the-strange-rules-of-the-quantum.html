Tiny things play by strange rules. Energy comes in chunks. A particle can be in a blend of states (superposition) until you look, and looking picks one outcome at random and changes the particle. Possibilities behave like waves that can add up or cancel (interference). Two particles can share one joint state (entanglement). You can't copy an unknown quantum state. And the environment is constantly 'peeking', which destroys the weirdness (decoherence).

You don't need to be a physicist to use a quantum computer, but you do need to accept a handful of rules that nature follows at the smallest scales. Here they are, in plain language.

3.1Rule 1: Things come in discrete chunks ("quanta")

In 1900, Max Planck found that energy is emitted in tiny indivisible packets called quanta. An electron in an atom can only sit at certain energy levels, like rungs on a ladder, never between them. Light is made of packets called photons. This "chunkiness" is where the word quantum comes from, and it's what lets us pick out two clean levels to serve as 0 and 1 of a qubit.

3.2Rule 2: Superposition

A quantum object can exist in a combination of several states at once. An electron's spin can be "up", "down", or a superposition of both. A photon can travel through two paths at once.

💡 Lightbulb moment

Analogy: the spinning coin. A coin lying flat is heads or tails (like a bit). A spinning coin is, in a sense, both at once until it lands. That's superposition.
Where the analogy breaks: a spinning coin really is one or the other at every instant; we just don't know which. A qubit in superposition is not secretly 0 or 1. It's genuinely in a new kind of state, and that state can interfere with itself (Rule 4), which a coin never does.

🤓 Did you know?

The 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger for experiments showing entanglement beats any hidden-variable explanation.

3.3Rule 3: Measurement gives random results and disturbs the system

When you measure a quantum system, you always get one definite outcome, chosen at random with probabilities set by the state (the Born rule). After measurement, the superposition is gone and the system is left in the state you observed. This is often called collapse.

So you can't "peek" at a superposition to see all its parts. Every measurement gives just one answer, and it changes the state.

3.4Rule 4: Interference

This is the most important rule for computing. Quantum possibilities behave like waves: each has an amplitude that can be positive, negative or complex. When two paths lead to the same outcome, their amplitudes add, and they can reinforce (constructive interference) or cancel (destructive interference).

The double-slit experiment shows this vividly. Fire single electrons, one at a time, at a wall with two slits. Each electron lands at a single spot on the detector screen, like a particle. But after thousands of electrons, the spots form stripes, an interference pattern, as if each electron went through both slits and interfered with itself. Cover one slit, and the stripes disappear.

💡 Lightbulb moment

Analogy: noise-cancelling headphones. They play a sound wave that is the exact opposite of the incoming noise. Peak meets trough, and the result is silence. Quantum algorithms do the same with computational paths: they arrange for paths to wrong answers to cancel, leaving the right answer loud and clear.

3.5Rule 5: Entanglement

Two or more quantum objects can be entangled: they share a single joint state, and measuring one instantly tells you about the other, no matter how far apart they are. Einstein called this "spooky action at a distance".

Example: two entangled qubits in the state "both 0 or both 1, in superposition". Measure the first: you randomly get 0 or 1 (50/50). The instant you do, you know the second will give the same result, even if it's on the other side of the galaxy.

💡 Lightbulb moment

Analogy: a pair of gloves. Put each glove of a pair in a separate box and ship one to Tokyo. Open your box, find a left glove, and you instantly know Tokyo has the right one.
Where the analogy breaks: with gloves, the answer was decided when you packed the boxes (a "hidden variable"). In 1964, John Bell proved that entangled particles produce correlations that no pre-decided hidden-variable explanation can reproduce, if you measure them in different directions. Experiments by Clauser, Aspect, Zeilinger and others confirmed quantum mechanics and violated Bell's inequality (Nobel Prize in Physics, 2022). Entanglement is genuinely stronger than any classical correlation.

🚫 Myth alert

Misconception: "Entanglement lets you send messages faster than light."
No. Each side sees purely random results on its own. The correlation only becomes visible when the two sides compare notes, and that comparison travels at light speed or slower. Relativity is safe.

3.6Rule 6: Uncertainty and complementarity

Some pairs of properties can't both be known precisely at the same time. The classic example is position and momentum (Heisenberg's uncertainty principle). For qubits, it means that if a qubit is perfectly definite in one "direction" (say, definitely 0), it is completely random in a complementary direction (Chapter 4.5). Quantum cryptography exploits this (Chapter 25).

3.7Rule 7: You can't copy an unknown quantum state

The no-cloning theorem (1982) says there's no machine that can take an arbitrary unknown quantum state and produce two perfect copies. This makes quantum computing harder (no simple backups, which complicates error correction) but quantum cryptography possible (eavesdroppers can't copy secrets). The proof is in Chapter 9.

3.8Rule 8: Decoherence, or why we don't see quantum weirdness every day

Quantum effects are fragile. When a quantum system interacts with its environment (stray light, heat, vibrations, a passing air molecule), information about its state leaks out. The environment effectively "measures" it, and the superposition is lost. This is decoherence, and it's why your coffee cup is never in two places at once. It's also the number-one enemy of quantum computers, which is why they are cooled close to absolute zero, held in vacuum, and shielded (Chapters 19 to 21).

⚖️ The quantum rulebook: superpowers and side effects

👍 The good
  • Superposition plus interference make a new kind of computation possible
  • Entanglement enables teleportation and key sharing protected by physics
  • No-cloning makes eavesdropping detectable
👎 The tricky
  • Measurement destroys superpositions: one answer per run
  • No-cloning means no simple backups
  • Decoherence: the environment ruins quantum states quickly

🎒 Pack these 3 ideas

  1. Superposition is a genuinely new kind of state, not 'secretly 0 or 1'.
  2. Interference is the engine of quantum computing.
  3. Entanglement gives correlations stronger than any classical plan, but it can't send messages faster than light.
