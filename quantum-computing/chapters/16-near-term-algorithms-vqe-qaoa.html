⚡ In 30 seconds (no jargon, promise)

Today's machines are noisy, so they can only run short circuits. The trick is teamwork: the quantum chip prepares a state using some adjustable knobs and measures its energy, then an ordinary computer nudges the knobs to lower the energy, and you repeat. It's like tuning a radio by ear. Other ideas include annealers (a ball rolling into valleys, sometimes tunnelling through hills) and quantum machine learning. Exciting, but no proven commercial advantage yet.

16.1The NISQ era

In 2018, John Preskill coined the term NISQ, for Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum: machines with roughly 50 to a few thousand qubits, too noisy for full error correction. Algorithms for NISQ machines must use short circuits and tolerate noise. Most combine a quantum computer with a classical one in a loop.

16.2The variational principle and the hybrid loop

The variational principle: for any trial state |ψ⟩, the measured energy ⟨ψ|H|ψ⟩ is always ≥ the true ground-state energy. So if you search for the trial state with the lowest energy, you approach the ground state from above.

✏️ Show the original text sketch
        ┌─────────────────────────── Classical computer ───────────────────────────┐
        │   Optimiser: "energy was −1.12; try parameters θ' = θ − η·gradient"      │
        └───────────────▲─────────────────────────────────────────┬────────────────┘
          energy E(θ)   │                                         │  new parameters θ
        ┌───────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────────▼────────────────┐
        │   Quantum computer: prepare |ψ(θ)⟩ with a parameterised circuit,         │
        │   measure many shots to estimate ⟨ψ(θ)|H|ψ(θ)⟩                           │
        └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                                 repeat until the energy stops decreasing
🧮 Pencil & paper

Toy example. Hamiltonian H = Z (ground state |1⟩, energy −1). Trial circuit Ry(θ)|0⟩.

  • Measured energy: E(θ) = ⟨Z⟩ = cos θ.
  • Minimum at θ = π, giving E = −1 ✓ (the state is |1⟩).
  • Gradients from the quantum computer itself (the "parameter-shift rule"): dE/dθ = [E(θ + π/2) − E(θ − π/2)] / 2 = [cos(θ + π/2) − cos(θ − π/2)] / 2 = −sin θ ✓. Run the same circuit at two shifted angles and subtract, with no calculus needed on the hardware.

16.3VQE: the Variational Quantum Eigensolver

Introduced in 2014 (Peruzzo et al.), VQE applies the loop above to molecular Hamiltonians:

  1. Map the molecule to a qubit Hamiltonian (a sum of Pauli strings).
  2. Choose an ansatz, a circuit template. It might be chemistry-inspired (like UCCSD) or "hardware-efficient" (layers of rotations and entanglers that suit the chip).
  3. Measure each Pauli term's expectation value, sum them into the energy, and let the optimiser update the parameters.

VQE has been run on H₂, LiH, BeH₂, small water clusters and more. Challenges: the number of measurements can be huge; optimisers get stuck; noise biases the energy; and for small molecules, classical computers solve the problem exactly anyway.

16.4QAOA: the Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm

Introduced in 2014 (Farhi, Goldstone and Gutmann), QAOA targets combinatorial optimisation: finding the best among exponentially many configurations.

Example problem: MaxCut. Colour each node of a graph black or white so that as many edges as possible connect different colours.

🧮 Pencil & paper

Worked example: a ring of 4 nodes (a square), edges 0–1, 1–2, 2–3, 3–0.

  • Colouring 0101 (alternating) cuts all 4 edges, which is the maximum.
  • Colouring 0011 cuts edges 1–2 and 3–0, so 2 edges.
  • Colouring 0000 cuts 0 edges.

QAOA alternates two kinds of layers, repeated p times:

  1. A cost layer that adds phases according to how good each colouring is.
  2. A mixer layer (X rotations) that shuffles amplitude between colourings.

The 2p angles are tuned by a classical optimiser. As p → ∞, QAOA can find the exact optimum (it approaches adiabatic computation). The open question is whether small p beats the best classical heuristics. So far, no convincing advantage over state-of-the-art classical solvers has been shown for practical problems.

16.5Quantum annealing

Quantum annealers such as those built by D-Wave are special-purpose machines, not general gate-based computers. Problems are encoded as finding the lowest-energy configuration of an Ising model (equivalently, a QUBO, a quadratic unconstrained binary optimisation problem).

💡 Lightbulb moment

Analogy: Imagine a ball rolling over a landscape of hills and valleys, looking for the deepest valley. A classical ball can get stuck in a shallow valley. A quantum ball can, in principle, tunnel through hills to reach deeper valleys.

The machine starts in an easy-to-prepare state (all qubits in superposition) and slowly morphs the energy landscape into the problem's landscape. If it's slow enough, the system ends up in or near the lowest-energy configuration.

Status: D-Wave's machines have thousands of qubits (its Advantage systems have over 5,000, and the Advantage2 generation launched in 2025 with improved connectivity and coherence). They're used in pilot projects for scheduling, logistics and materials simulation. In 2025 D-Wave published a Science paper claiming a beyond-classical simulation of quantum magnetic materials, though some classical-simulation groups challenged the claim. Whether annealers give a general speedup for optimisation remains unproven and debated.

16.6Quantum machine learning (QML)

Ideas include encoding data into quantum states and training parameterised quantum circuits as "quantum neural networks", using quantum kernels to measure similarity in a huge feature space, and quantum versions of linear-algebra subroutines.

Reality check:

  • Barren plateaus: for many circuit designs, gradients vanish exponentially with qubit count, making training impossible (McClean et al., 2018).
  • Data loading: classical data must be encoded into quantum states, which can erase any speedup.
  • Dequantisation: several celebrated quantum ML speedups were matched by new classical algorithms (Tang, 2018 and later).
  • The most promising direction is learning from quantum data, for example analysing the output of quantum sensors or experiments directly. Here provable advantages exist: Huang et al. demonstrated an exponential advantage in learning about quantum systems in 2022.

16.7Error mitigation: squeezing value from noisy hardware

Without full error correction, researchers use error mitigation techniques that reduce noise in averaged results:

TechniqueIdea
Zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE)Deliberately run the circuit at amplified noise levels (1×, 2×, 3×) and extrapolate the result back to zero noise
Probabilistic error cancellation (PEC)Learn the noise model, then randomly insert gates that statistically "undo" it, at the cost of many more shots
Readout error mitigationCalibrate how often 0 is misread as 1 (and vice versa), then correct the statistics
Symmetry verificationDiscard results that violate a known conservation law (e.g. particle number)
Dynamical decouplingInsert pulse sequences into idle periods to cancel slow noise

In 2023, IBM used ZNE on a 127-qubit processor to compute properties of a magnetic model at a scale they argued was beyond brute-force classical simulation (the "quantum utility" paper). Within weeks, several classical groups reproduced the results with tensor networks and other methods. Key limitation: mitigation costs grow exponentially with circuit size, so it's a bridge, not a replacement, for error correction.

16.8The honest verdict on NISQ algorithms

As of late 2026, there is no widely accepted demonstration of a commercially useful quantum advantage from NISQ algorithms. There are compelling scientific demonstrations, and steady progress, and the field's centre of gravity has shifted toward early error-corrected machines (Chapters 20 and 29).

⚖️ NISQ algorithms

👍 The good
  • Run on today's hardware with short circuits
  • Hybrid loops tolerate some noise
  • Error mitigation squeezes out extra accuracy
👎 The tricky
  • Huge numbers of measurements, and optimisers that get stuck
  • Barren plateaus make gradients vanish at scale
  • Classical methods often match the results (e.g. the 2023 'utility' paper)

🎒 Pack these 3 ideas

  1. Variational principle: the measured energy is always ≥ the true ground-state energy.
  2. Parameter-shift rule: get gradients from two runs of the same circuit at shifted angles.
  3. No widely accepted, commercially useful NISQ advantage as of late 2026.
