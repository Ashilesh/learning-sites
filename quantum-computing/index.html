⏱ Quantum in 60 seconds

Tap the right side to skip ahead, the left side to go back. Hold to pause.

💡

Your computer thinks in bits

Every photo, song and website is billions of tiny switches, each 0 or 1. Only one value at a time.

🪙

Nature thinks in qubits

A qubit can be in a blend of 0 and 1 at once. That's superposition, and it's not 'secretly one or the other'.

🧤

Qubits can be linked

Entangled qubits share one joint state. Measure one, and you instantly know what the other will say.

🌊

Possibilities are waves

Each possibility has an amplitude that can add up or cancel out. That's interference, the engine of every quantum algorithm.

👀

Looking changes everything

Measuring gives one random answer and destroys the blend. So algorithms must make wrong answers cancel before you look.

🧪

So what's it good for?

Simulating molecules, factoring huge numbers, some kinds of search. Not Netflix, not spreadsheets.

🫧

The catch

Qubits are fragile. Error correction spreads one reliable qubit across hundreds of physical ones.

🚀

Where we are (late 2026)

Error correction now works below threshold. Useful advantage? Not yet. Let's dive in.

📐 How to read this guide

This guide is meant to be your one-stop resource for quantum computing. It starts with zero assumptions and builds up, chapter by chapter, to the same topics covered in university courses and research papers: algorithms, error correction, hardware, cryptography, and the current state of the industry.

Pick a reading track: the three track cards above, from the guide's own table.

Conventions used throughout:

💡 Lightbulb moment

Analogy: an everyday comparison to build intuition. Analogies are never perfect, so we'll point out where they break.

🚫 Myth alert

Misconception: a popular but wrong idea, and the correct version.

🧮 Worked example

Worked example: step-by-step calculation you can follow with pencil and paper.

🔬 Deeper dive · optional

Deeper dive: optional detail for readers who want more. Safe to skip.

Notation: We write quantum states like |0⟩ and |ψ⟩. The symbol |…⟩ is called a "ket" and is pronounced "ket zero", "ket psi", and so on. It's just a label that says "this is a quantum state". Chapter 5 explains it fully.

Quantum computing moves fast. The fundamentals in Parts I to III are timeless, but hardware numbers, records and roadmaps (Parts IV and VII) should be re-checked against current sources. The 2026 results in this guide come from company announcements and preprints, so treat them as reported claims until they're independently confirmed.
