Chapter 1 · The four secret-keeping jobs

Cryptography does four jobs for you, all day long

The new quantum-safe locks don't change these jobs. They only replace the tools a quantum computer could break.

Keep it secret

Like a sealed envelope only your friend can open.

Real life: your bank balance travels scrambled with AES-GCM.

Spot tampering

Like the seal on a medicine bottle. If it's broken, you know.

Real life: a 16-byte AEAD tag on every TLS record.

Prove who you are

Like checking someone's ID card at the door.

Real life: mybank.com's certificate, signed by a Certificate Authority.

No take-backs

Like signing a contract in front of a witness. You can't say "that wasn't me".

Real life: a signed app update from your phone's maker.

Like you're eight

One key for one box (symmetric): you and your friend each have a copy of the same key. Fast! But how do you hand your friend the key without a bully grabbing it?

Open padlocks (public key): you give out open padlocks to everyone. Anyone can snap one shut on a box, but only you have the key to open it.

A special stamp (signature): only you can make your stamp, but everyone can check it's really yours.

In real life

Your phone uses both. It uses the slow padlock kind for a split second to agree a secret with the website, then switches to the fast one-key kind (AES or ChaCha20) for everything else: pictures, passwords, videos.

The rule to remember: anything with a public key is in quantum trouble. One-key locks and fingerprints (hashes) are fine, as long as they're big enough (256 bits).

Chapter 3 · Meet the quantum robot

It isn't a faster laptop. It's a master key for one kind of lock.

A quantum computer is brilliant at a few special maths puzzles. Sadly, two of those puzzles hold up most of the internet's security.

Like you're eight

Shor's trick (1994) is like a robot that can un-mix paint. Every padlock lock (RSA, Diffie-Hellman, elliptic curves) pops open. Making the lock bigger doesn't help, because the robot has a master key, not a crowbar.

Grover's trick (1996) only helps the robot guess faster. A 256-bit key acts like a 128-bit key. That's still more guesses than there are grains of sand on every beach, times a lot more.

In real life

Breaking RSA-2048 needs thousands of reliable "logical" qubits. A 2025 estimate by Craig Gidney at Google put it under one million physical qubits running for about a week, down from about 20 million in earlier estimates.

Today's machines have hundreds to a few thousand physical qubits. Most experts point to the 2030s, and governments plan for the early end.

Chapter 4 · Harvest now, decrypt later

The sneaky collector is already photocopying locked letters

The robot doesn't exist yet. But someone can record scrambled messages today and keep them in a drawer until it does. That's why this matters now.

Like you're eight

Imagine someone copies every locked diary page you pass to a friend, and keeps the copies in a big jar. Today they can't read them. In ten years, if they get a magic key, they open the whole jar at once.

So the secrets that must stay secret for a long time are already in danger.

In real life

Hospitals keep patient records for decades. Genetic data, state secrets, source code and contracts stay valuable for years. Encrypted traffic carrying them can be recorded at internet exchanges, cloud links or Wi-Fi today.

Fixing key exchange stops this. Signatures are less urgent, because faking a signature only helps while it still matters, so the attacker needs the robot at that moment.

Am I already late? (Mosca's rule)

If the years your data must stay secret plus the years your upgrade takes are more than the years until the robot arrives, you're late.

Starting values are the guide's hospital example: 30-year records, 5-year upgrade, robot in 20 years.

Chapter 5 · The new locks

New maths puzzles the robot isn't good at

"Post-quantum" locks are ordinary software on ordinary computers. They're built on puzzles with no known quantum shortcut. NIST published the first three as standards in August 2024.

Try it: the wobbly treasure map (Learning With Errors)

The treasure sits on one dot of the grid. You're given its location with a little wobble added. Click the dot you think is the treasure. Turn the wobble up and see how hard it gets.

Why it's hard for the robot too

On a flat map with two directions, a little wobble is easy to undo: you just pick the nearest dot.

ML-KEM's map has 768 directions at once (3 blocks of 256), with wrap-around maths using the number 3329. The number of dots "near" the wobbly point is so big that no computer, quantum or not, is known to search it.

Grown-up version: the public key is t = A·s + e. The secret s hides behind a small error e. Remove e and it's school algebra. Keep it, and it's believed to be hard.

Chapter 6 · Bigger luggage

The new locks aren't slower. They're heavier.

ML-KEM is about as fast as X25519, but its messages are about 35 times bigger. Most real-world headaches come from size, not speed. Bars below are drawn to scale.

A whole TLS handshake

Roughly what the server and browser send before your page loads. TCP sends about 14 KB before it has to wait for a reply; going over costs an extra trip.

That's why the web switched key exchange first (adds about 2.3 KB in total) and is still designing smaller ways to do PQ certificates, like Merkle Tree Certificates.

Chapter 7 · Cipher suites, decoded

A cipher suite is an order at a sandwich shop

Each part of the name says which ingredient goes where. Learn to read the name and you can tell, at a glance, whether a connection would survive the quantum robot.

Like you're eight

Picture ordering: "Secret-handshake bread, ID-card filling, AES-256 lock, GCM seal, SHA-384 fingerprint." Each word is one job:

Handshake: how we agree on a secret · ID card: how the shop proves it's the real shop · Lock: what scrambles the sandwich · Seal: how we spot if someone took a bite · Fingerprint: the maths that mixes keys.

The big change in TLS 1.3

In TLS 1.2 the whole order was one long name, like TLS_ECDHE_RSA_WITH_AES_256_GCM_SHA384.

In TLS 1.3 the name only covers the lock, seal and fingerprint, like TLS_AES_128_GCM_SHA256. The handshake and ID card are picked from two separate menus: supported_groups and signature_algorithms. So the internet could add a quantum-safe handshake without changing any cipher suite name.

What this tells us, kid size

Most big front doors now have the new double lock. About two out of three sites we tried used the quantum-safe handshake, close to Cloudflare's own number.

Nobody has a quantum-safe ID card yet. Every certificate was still RSA or ECDSA, because PQ certificates for the web aren't ready.

Some doors are still old. A few big banks, payment and government sites still talk TLS 1.2, and the code-hosting SSH servers still use a classical handshake. A collector could record those today.

How we measured: openssl s_client -connect <site>:443 -servername <site> -showcerts with default settings, and ssh -vv git@<host> with authentication turned off. One measurement from one location; big sites can answer differently depending on which server you reach. The cipher suite partly reflects OpenSSL's own preference order, so a browser may see a different one; the handshake group and certificate would be the same.

Chapter 11 · The plan

Changing every lock in a city, in the right order

Governments set the dates: NIST says old public-key locks are discouraged by 2030 and banned by 2035. The EU wants high-risk systems protected by 2030. Companies follow eight phases.

Five rules, kid size

1. Find every lock in the house before you change any. 2. Fix the locks the collector is copying first (key exchange). 3. Use locks that are easy to swap next time. 4. While you're switching, use two locks on the door (hybrid). 5. Do the most important doors first, not the easiest.

In real life: a payments company

Do now: turn on X25519MLKEM768 at the load balancer, upgrade Envoy and the service mesh, upgrade to OpenSSH 10. Plan: move the HSM that signs settlement files to ML-DSA or LMS, ask partner banks about PQ PGP, re-wrap database keys. Keep for now: 10-minute JWTs signed with ES256, with room for bigger tokens later.
