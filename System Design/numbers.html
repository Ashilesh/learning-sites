3.1

🛣️Latency, throughput & bandwidth

⏳ Latency

How long one trip takes. “My order took 3 seconds.” Lower is better.

🚚 Throughput

How many trips finish per second. “We serve 5,000 orders/sec.” Higher is better.

🛣️ Bandwidth

The max capacity of the road — how many lanes. Throughput can't go above it.
–latency (one car)
–throughput

More lanes → more cars per second, but each trip takes the same time. A shorter road → faster trips.

🧠 Little's Law (a tiny superpower)

Things in the system = arrival rate × time each spends inside. If 1,000 requests/sec arrive and each takes 0.2 s, about 200 requests are in flight at once — so you need room for ~200 concurrent connections.

3.2

🐌Latency numbers everyone should know

Computers are fast, but distance and disks are slow. Flip the switch to stretch time so 1 nanosecond = 1 second.

Bars use a log scale — each step is ~10× longer.
👶 The lesson

Reading memory is like grabbing a snack. Asking a server across the ocean is like a 5-year trip around the world. That's why we use caches and put servers near users!

3.3

🍦Percentiles: p50, p90, p99

100 kids wait in line for ice cream. Line them up from fastest to slowest service:

  • p50 (median) = the wait of kid #50. Half waited less.
  • p90 = kid #90. Only 10 waited longer.
  • p99 = kid #99. The unlucky 1 in 100. 😤
⚠️ Why not just the average?

Averages hide pain. If 99 kids wait 1 minute and one waits 60, the average is ~1.6 min — “looks fine!” — but that one kid is crying. Big companies watch p99 because their most active users hit it the most.

3.4

🎯The tail at scale: why p99 matters MORE in big systems

One page often calls many services at once (profile, feed, ads, friends…). The page is only as fast as the slowest one.

If each service is slow just 1% of the time (its p99)…

chance a page is slow
10%

🧠 Fixes for tail latency

Set timeouts, send a hedged request to a second server if the first is slow, keep caches warm, avoid huge garbage-collection pauses, and reduce how many services each request needs.

3.6

📜SLI, SLO, SLA & error budgets

📏 SLI — Indicator

What you measure. “95% of pizzas arrived in 28 minutes this week.”

🎯 SLO — Objective

Your internal goal. “95% of pizzas within 30 minutes.”

🤝 SLA — Agreement

A promise with a penalty. “Late? Your pizza is free!” (SLA is looser than the SLO.)

💸 Error budget: how much failure you're allowed

An SLO of 99.9% over 30 days lets you be down 43 minutes. That's your budget to spend on risky launches. Spent it? Stop shipping features and fix reliability.

3.7

🧮Back-of-the-envelope estimation

Before designing, estimate! Rough numbers tell you if you need 1 server or 1,000. Play with this calculator for a photo-sharing app:

⏰ Time cheats

  • 1 day ≈ 86,400 s ≈ 105 s
  • 1 million/day ≈ 12 per second
  • 1 billion/day ≈ 12,000 per second
  • 1 month ≈ 2.5 million s

💾 Size cheats

  • 1 char ≈ 1 byte · tweet ≈ 300 B
  • Photo ≈ 200 KB–2 MB
  • 1 min HD video ≈ 50–100 MB
  • KB → MB → GB → TB → PB (×1000 each)

🖥️ Capacity rules of thumb

  • Web server: ~1K–10K simple req/s
  • SQL DB: ~5K–20K simple queries/s
  • Redis: ~100K+ ops/s per node
  • Use 2–3× headroom for peaks

Rules of thumb vary a lot with hardware and workload — they're for quick sizing, not promises.
